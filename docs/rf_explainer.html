Fooling a radio's fingerprint

Every wireless device hides a hardware fingerprint inside its signal, and a neural network can read it to tell devices apart. Here is how a second radio forges that fingerprint over the air, without ever touching the message.

↓ scroll

01 · The fingerprint

Same message, different signature

Send the exact same WiFi packet from two different transmitters and the bits come out identical. The analog hardware does not: small imperfections in each radio's oscillator, mixer, and power amplifier stamp a consistent distortion onto every signal it sends.

Train a network on recordings from five of our devices and it learns to name which one is transmitting from that distortion alone, not from the content. It is the difference between reading what someone wrote and recognizing their handwriting.

02 · The attack

A whisper that rewrites the signature

An adversarial attack adds a tiny, carefully shaped perturbation to the signal. It is small enough that the WiFi receiver still decodes every bit correctly, but it is aimed to push the classifier's decision across a boundary. The message arrives intact; the classifier reports the wrong device.

03 · Over the air

The attack does not live in a computer

In Experiment 3 the perturbation is transmitted. The victim device sends its signal from one radio; a second radio, the adversary, sends the perturbation from a different antenna. The two combine in the air and reach the receiver together, which runs the classifier. Nothing is edited after capture — the attack happens physically, on the channel.

Two USRP radios, MIMO-locked so they share a clock; receiver at 5.29 GHz. The adversary never sees the victim's carrier phase.

04 · The surprising part

05 · Why we study it

If a fingerprint can be forged, it is not a password

RF fingerprinting is proposed as a way to authenticate devices by their hardware, something an attacker supposedly cannot copy. Experiment 3 shows a weak, separate radio can make the classifier read whatever identity it chooses, over the air, without breaking the link.

So the work is about the boundaries of that:

  • where the attack works, and how little power it really needs
  • what quietly gives it away — the frames the receiver starts dropping once the adversary pushes too hard
  • how a receiver might tell impersonation apart from ordinary interference
